Advanced Clauses

Connect ideas precisely with noun clauses, advanced relative clauses, adverbial clauses, and reduced clauses. Study examples, practise, then test yourself.

1. What is a clause?

A clause is a group of words built around a verb. It usually contains a subject and a verb.

Independent clause

Can stand alone

The train arrived.

It expresses a complete thought.

Dependent clause

Needs a main clause

because the train was late

It leaves the reader waiting for the rest.

Combined: We missed the meeting because the train was late.

Advanced writing often uses one main clause and one or more dependent clauses to show how ideas relate.

2. Noun clauses

A noun clause does a job normally done by a noun: it can be a subject, object, or subject complement.

Subject

What she decided surprised us.

The clause is the subject of “surprised.”

Object

I believe that he is honest.

The clause is the object of “believe.”

Complement

The problem is that we are late.

The clause explains “the problem.”

Whether / if

We do not know whether the shop is open.

These express uncertainty or yes/no meaning.

That can often be omitted after think, say, and know: “I think (that) you're right.”
Embedded questions use statement word order:
✓ I wonder where he is.
✗ I wonder where is he.

3. Advanced relative clauses

A relative clause describes a noun or gives extra information about it.

Defining

The student who won the prize is my cousin.

Identifies which student; no commas.

Non-defining

My cousin, who lives in Delhi, is visiting.

Adds extra information; use commas.

Whose

The author whose book I read won an award.

Shows possession.

Where / when / why

This is the café where we met.

Place, time, and reason respectively.

Omitting the relative pronoun: “The film (that) we watched was excellent.” You may omit that/which/who when it is the object, but not when it is the subject: “The person who called left a message.”
Prepositions: Formal: “the colleague to whom I spoke.” Conversational: “the colleague who I spoke to.”
Important: Non-defining relative clauses normally cannot use that: “My laptop, which I bought last year, is slow.”

4. Adverbial clauses

These clauses explain when, why, under what condition, or in what way something happens.

Time

When the bell rang, we left.

when, while, before, after, until, once

Reason

We stayed inside because it was raining.

because, since, as

Condition

If you practise, you will improve.

if, unless, provided that

Concession

Although it was late, they continued.

although, though, even though

Purpose

She spoke slowly so that everyone could understand.

so that, in order that

Result / comparison

It was so cold that the lake froze.

so…that; than/as…as patterns

Although vs. despite: “Although it was raining, we went out.” (clause) / “Despite the rain, we went out.” (noun phrase). Also: “Despite being tired, she worked.”

Punctuation: Put a comma after an introductory adverbial clause. If the main clause comes first, a comma is often unnecessary: “We left when the bell rang.”

Reduced clauses and participle clauses

Some relative or adverbial clauses can be shortened when the meaning remains clear and the subject relationship is understood.

Full: The woman who is standing by the door is our teacher.
Reduced: The woman standing by the door is our teacher.
Full: Because he felt exhausted, Amir went to bed early.
Reduced: Feeling exhausted, Amir went to bed early.
Purpose infinitive: She saved money to buy a laptop.
Avoid dangling modifiers: “Walking to class, the rain started” incorrectly suggests the rain was walking. Better: “Walking to class, I got caught in the rain.” The understood subject of the opening phrase should match the subject that follows.
