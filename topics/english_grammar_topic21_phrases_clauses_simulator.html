Phrases and Clauses

Learn how groups of words work together to build clear sentences.

Phrase vs. clause

A phrase is a group of words that works together but does not contain a complete subject–finite verb combination.

A clause contains a subject and a verb. Some clauses can stand alone as complete sentences; others depend on another clause.

PHRASE

under the old bridge

No subject + finite verb combination.

CLAUSE

the bridge collapsed

Subject: bridge · Verb: collapsed.

Not every clause is a full sentence. “Because I was tired” has a subject and verb, but it leaves the reader waiting for the main idea.

Common types of phrases

Noun phrase
The very clever student answered.
Works like a noun.
Verb phrase
She has been studying all day.
Main verb and auxiliaries.
Adjective phrase
The answer was extremely useful.
Describes a noun or gives a quality.
Adverb phrase
He spoke very quietly.
Modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb.
Prepositional phrase
The keys are on the table.
Preposition + its object and related words.
Infinitive phrase
She hopes to win the prize.
Infinitive plus its complements.
Gerund phrase
Reading before bed relaxes me.
An -ing form used like a noun.
Participial phrase
Exhausted by the journey, he slept.
A participle phrase describing a noun.

Some grammar books classify phrases differently. Focus first on the group's job in the sentence.

Independent and dependent clauses

INDEPENDENT

The train arrived.

A complete thought; can stand alone.

DEPENDENT

because the train was late

Has a subject and verb but needs a main clause.

Combine them: We missed the meeting because the train was late.

Other examples

Although it was raining, we went outside.
Dependent: Although it was raining · Independent: we went outside.
I know that she is honest.
Independent: I know · Dependent noun clause: that she is honest.
The book that you gave me is useful.
Independent/main clause includes “The book … is useful”; relative clause: that you gave me.

Three common dependent clause types

Noun clause: works like a noun.
What you said surprised me.
The clause is the subject of “surprised.”
Adjective / relative clause: describes a noun.
The student who won smiled.
Adverb clause: gives information such as time, reason, condition, or contrast.
We stayed inside because it was cold.

Common subordinators

Time: when, before, after, while · Reason: because, since · Condition: if, unless · Contrast: although, even though · Purpose: so that.

Be careful: a word like “that” can introduce different structures. Identify the clause's role in context.
