Relative Clauses

Join ideas and describe people, places, and things more precisely.

What is a relative clause?

A relative clause gives extra information about a noun. It often begins with who, whom, whose, which, or that.

The girl who won the competition is my friend.

“Who won the competition” tells us which girl. It combines information that could be expressed as two sentences:

The girl won the competition. She is my friend.
→ The girl who won the competition is my friend.

The noun being described is called the antecedent (here, “girl”).

Choosing the relative word

WordUsed forExample
whoPeople; subject of the clauseThe student who called is here.
whomPeople; object, especially formal EnglishThe person whom I met was kind.
whosePossession; people and sometimes thingsThe woman whose bag was lost called.
whichThings and animalsThe book which fell is mine.
thatPeople or things in defining clausesThe film that we watched was funny.
wherePlaceThis is the café where we met.
whenTimeI remember the day when we met.
whyReason (often after “reason”)I know the reason why she left.

Tip: “Who” usually does the action; “whom” receives it. In everyday English, “who” is also common as an object, especially in informal speech.

Defining vs. non-defining clauses

DEFINING

The students who studied passed the test.

Identifies which students. No commas.

NON-DEFINING

My brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.

Adds extra information. Use commas.

In standard English, that is not used in non-defining clauses. Non-defining clauses generally do not allow the relative pronoun to be omitted.

Compare the meaning

“My cousins who live in Mumbai are visiting.” suggests that the cousins who live in Mumbai are the ones visiting (not necessarily all cousins).

“My cousins, who live in Mumbai, are visiting.” presents the Mumbai detail as extra information about the cousins being discussed.

When can the relative word be omitted?

You can often omit who, whom, which, or that when it is the object of a defining relative clause.

The movie (that) we watched was excellent. (“That” is the object of “watched.”)
The person who called is waiting. (“Who” is the subject, so do not omit it.)

Do not omit the relative word in a non-defining clause: “My car, which I bought last year, is blue.”

Punctuation and prepositions

  • Use commas around non-defining clauses: “My laptop, which I bought last year, still works.”
  • In formal English: “the colleague to whom I spoke.” In everyday English: “the colleague who I spoke to.”
  • Use whose for possession: “the author whose book won.”
