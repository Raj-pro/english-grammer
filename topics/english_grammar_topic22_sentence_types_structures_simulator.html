Sentence Types and Structures

Learn how sentences are built and how we use them to communicate different purposes.

Two ways to classify sentences

We can classify sentences by purpose (what the speaker wants to do) or by structure (how clauses are combined). These are two different systems.

PURPOSE

Are you ready?

Interrogative: asks a question.

STRUCTURE

Although I was tired, I finished the task.

Complex: one independent clause + one dependent clause.

A sentence may have both a purpose label and a structure label. For example, a complex sentence can also be interrogative.

Four sentence purposes

Declarative
She works in a hospital.
Makes a statement; usually ends with a full stop.
Interrogative
Does she work here?
Asks a question; usually ends with a question mark.
Imperative
Please take a seat.
Gives a command, instruction, request, or advice.
Exclamatory
What a wonderful view!
Expresses strong feeling; ends with an exclamation mark.

Imperatives often have an understood subject you: “(You) listen carefully.” A sentence ending in “!” is not automatically a different grammatical structure; punctuation and purpose are related but not identical.

Four sentence structures

Simple: I read every night.
One independent clause.
Compound: I was tired, but I finished my work.
Two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction.
Complex: I finished my work although I was tired.
One independent clause + at least one dependent clause.
Compound-complex: I was tired, but I finished my work because it was urgent.
At least two independent clauses + at least one dependent clause.

Coordinating conjunctions: FANBOYS

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So can join independent clauses. Use a comma before the conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.

Common sentence patterns

PatternExample
Subject + verbBirds fly.
Subject + verb + objectShe reads books.
Subject + linking verb + complementHe is happy.
Subject + verb + indirect object + direct objectShe gave me a gift.
Subject + verb + object + object complementThey elected him captain.

Sentence fragments and run-ons

Fragment: Because I missed the bus.
Complete: Because I missed the bus, I arrived late.
Run-on: I was tired I went to bed.
Correct: I was tired, so I went to bed. / I was tired; I went to bed.

A semicolon can join closely related independent clauses. A comma alone should not join two independent clauses (comma splice).
