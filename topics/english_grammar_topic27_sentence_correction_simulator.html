Sentence Correction

Learn to identify errors, select the best correction, and improve unclear or awkward sentences. Use a repeatable editing method, then test your skills with 40 multiple-choice questions.

1. A reliable sentence-correction method

When correcting a sentence, look for the actual problem first. Do not rewrite everything if one small change fixes it.

Step 1 — Find the subject

Identify who or what performs the action or is described.

Step 2 — Check the verb

Check agreement, tense, and verb form.

Step 3 — Check word choices

Look at articles, pronouns, prepositions, and countability.

Step 4 — Check structure

Look for fragments, run-ons, parallelism, and word order.

Step 5 — Check meaning

Make sure the correction says what the writer intends.

Step 6 — Read it aloud

Check whether the sentence is clear, complete, and natural.

Minimum-change principle

In grammar exercises, the best answer often makes the smallest change that fixes the error while preserving the original meaning.

Original: She don't likes coffee.
Corrected: She doesn't like coffee.
Changes: don't → doesn't; likes → like. After doesn't, use the base verb.
Original: Because I missed the bus.
Corrected: I was late because I missed the bus.
Change: Add an independent clause to complete the thought.

Choosing among answer options

  • Eliminate choices that introduce a new error.
  • Prefer a correction that preserves the original meaning.
  • Watch for unnecessary words such as “return back” or “repeat again.”
  • Do not confuse a stylistic preference with a genuine grammar error; several sentences may be grammatical.
  • In formal exam questions, follow standard written English and the context provided.
“The reason is because I was late” is common in speech, but a more concise formal version is “The reason is that I was late” or simply “I was late because…”

3. Clarity, concision, and meaning

Remove redundancy

“Return back” → “return”
“Repeat again” → “repeat”

Avoid vague pronouns

Unclear: When Anu met Priya, she was upset.
Clear: Anu was upset when she met Priya.

Prefer precise words

Weak: He did a decision.
Clear: He made a decision.

Keep comparisons logical

Incorrect: My laptop is faster than Rahul.
Clear: My laptop is faster than Rahul's laptop.

Dangling and misplaced modifiers

A modifier should clearly describe the word it is intended to modify.

Misplaced: She almost drove her children to school every day. (Suggests she nearly drove them, but perhaps did not.)
Clearer: She drove her children to school almost every day.
Dangling: Walking to class, the rain began.
Clearer: While I was walking to class, it began to rain.

Parallelism and comparisons

  • Keep list items in matching forms: “to read, to write, and to speak” or “reading, writing, and speaking.”
  • Compare like with like: “The salary of this job is higher than that of my old job.”
  • Use fewer with countable plural nouns and less with uncountable nouns in standard formal writing.
  • Use “between you and me,” not “between you and I,” because the preposition takes an object pronoun.
