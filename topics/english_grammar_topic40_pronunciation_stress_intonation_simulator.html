Pronunciation, Word Stress & Intonation

Learn how sounds, emphasis, rhythm, and pitch make spoken English easier to understand.

1. Word stress

One syllable in a multi-syllable word often sounds stronger, longer, or clearer.

First syllable

TA-ble

Second syllable

be-GIN

Stress can change meaning

RE-cord (noun)
re-CORD (verb)

Tip: Learn a word's pronunciation and stress together. Stress patterns have tendencies, but there are exceptions.

2. Sentence stress

Important words often receive more emphasis than small grammar words.

I NEED a NEW PHONE.
  • SHE sent the email, not me.
  • She sent the EMAIL, not the letter.
  • She SENT the email; she didn't draft it.

Moving the stress changes what information sounds most important.

3. Intonation: the rise and fall of your voice

Rising ↗

Are you ready↗?

Often used in yes/no questions, uncertainty, or continuation.

Falling ↘

Where are you going↘?

Often used for neutral wh-questions and completed statements.

These are common patterns, not strict rules. Context, emotion, and regional speech habits affect intonation.

4. Rhythm, pauses, and clear sounds

  • Group words into meaningful phrases.
  • Pause between ideas, not inside words.
  • Speak at a comfortable pace and finish word endings.
  • Small words such as “a” and “to” may be reduced when unstressed.
  • Keep your mouth relaxed; clarity matters more than copying someone else's accent.

A simple 10-minute routine

  1. 2 minutes — Listen: choose a short English sentence from a trusted audio source.
  2. 2 minutes — Notice: identify stressed words and pauses.
  3. 3 minutes — Imitate: repeat slowly, then at a natural pace.
  4. 2 minutes — Record: optionally use your phone or computer's recorder.
  5. 1 minute — Review: choose one thing to improve tomorrow.
Today's practice sentence:
“Could you please send me the report before lunch?”
Try stressing SEND first, then BEFORE LUNCH. Notice how emphasis changes.

Short, regular practice is easier to sustain than a long session once in a while.
