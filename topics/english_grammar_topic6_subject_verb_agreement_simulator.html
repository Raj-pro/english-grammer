Subject–Verb Agreement

Learn how a verb changes to match its subject: “She works” but “They work.” Explore simple rules, tricky examples, an interactive sentence builder, and 40 practice questions.

What is subject–verb agreement?

The subject tells us who or what the sentence is about. The verb tells us an action or state. In the present simple, the verb must match its subject.

The boy runs every day. One boy → runs
The boys run every day. More than one → run
Core rule: A singular third-person subject (he, she, it, one person or thing) usually takes a verb ending in -s/-es in the present simple. Plural subjects and I/you usually take the base form.

Present simple quick chart

I / You / We / TheyI work. You work. We work. They work.
He / She / ItHe works. She works. It works.
Be verbI am; he/she/it is; you/we/they are.
Have / DoShe has / does. They have / do.

More examples

My friend likes music. My friends like music. The bus arrives at eight. The buses arrive at eight.

Rules to remember

1. Add -s/-es for he, she, itShe reads. He watches. The baby cries.
2. Do not add -s with I/you/we/theyThey play. We study. You know.
3. Modal + base verbShe can swim. He should rest. (Not “can swims”.)
4. Has / haveHe has a key. They have keys.
5. Do / doesDoes she sing? They do not sing. Use the base verb after does/do.
6. Be verbsI am; he is; they are. Past: I/he was; you/we/they were.
In past simple, most action verbs do not change with the subject: “I walked,” “she walked,” “they walked.” But “be” changes: was/were.

Singular or plural?

A singular noun means one; a plural noun usually means more than one. Find the subject first, then choose the matching verb.

SingularThe dog barks. My teacher explains. A child plays.
PluralThe dogs bark. My teachers explain. Children play.
Two subjects with “and”Riya and Sam are friends.
Each / everyEach student has a book. Every room is clean.

Try it: choose the right form

The cat ___ on the sofa.

Tricky subject rules

Each / every / everyoneEach student is ready. Everyone knows the rule.
Either / neitherNeither answer is correct. (Beginner rule: treat either/neither alone as singular.)
There is / there areThere is a book. There are two books.
Uncountable nounsThe information is useful. The furniture looks new.
Collective nounsThe team is ready. (Common in American English when the team is one unit.)
Words between subject and verbThe box of pencils is heavy. Subject = box, not pencils.
One of + plural nounOne of my friends lives nearby. Subject = one.
As well as / along withRavi, along with his cousins, is coming. The main subject stays Ravi.
Tip: Cross out extra information temporarily. “The box of pencils is heavy.” The main subject is still “box.”

Common errors

Wrong: Everyone know this. → Correct: Everyone knows this.
Wrong: The list of names are long. → Correct: The list of names is long.
Wrong: She can speaks English. → Correct: She can speak English.
Wrong: There is two chairs. → Correct: There are two chairs.

Fast checklist

  1. Find the real subject.
  2. Decide whether it is singular or plural.
  3. Check the tense.
  4. For present simple, add -s/-es for he/she/it.
  5. After can, should, does, or did, use the base verb where appropriate.
