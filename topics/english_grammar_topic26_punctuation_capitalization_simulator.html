Punctuation and Capitalization

Make your writing clearer and easier to read. Learn full stops, commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, quotation marks, and capitalization, then practise editing sentences.

1. The main punctuation marks

Full stop / period (.)

Ends a statement.

I study English.
Question mark (?)

Ends a direct question.

Where do you live?
Exclamation mark (!)

Shows strong emotion or emphasis; use sparingly in formal writing.

What a surprise!
Comma (,)

Separates items, introductory phrases, and some clauses.

After lunch, we went out.
Semicolon (;)

Joins closely related independent clauses or separates complex list items.

It was late; we went home.
Colon (:)

Introduces an explanation, example, or list after a complete clause.

Bring three things: a pen, paper, and a ruler.
Apostrophe (')

Shows possession or missing letters in contractions.

Mira's bag; don't worry.
Quotation marks (“ ”)

Mark direct speech or quoted words.

She said, “I'm ready.”
Hyphen (-) / dash (—)

A hyphen connects parts of some compound words; a dash marks a break or emphasis.

a well-known writer — and a good teacher

Semicolon vs. colon

Semicolon: The road was flooded; we took another route. Both sides can stand as sentences.
Colon: I packed the essentials: water, snacks, and a map. The words before the colon form a complete introduction.

Avoid placing a colon directly between a verb or preposition and its object: usually not “I bought: apples and pears.” Write “I bought apples and pears” or “I bought two things: apples and pears.”

2. Commas: common uses

  • Lists: I bought apples, rice, and milk. The final comma (Oxford comma) is common in many styles and can prevent ambiguity; follow your style guide consistently.
  • Introductory elements: After the meeting, we went for lunch.
  • Direct address: Raj, please check this. / Thank you, everyone.
  • Non-essential information: My brother, who lives in Delhi, is visiting. The clause adds extra information.
  • Before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses: I was tired, so I went home.
  • Dates and places: In American style, commas often separate day/year and city/state; styles differ internationally.
A comma alone normally cannot join two complete sentences. Incorrect: “I was late, I missed the bus.” Correct: “I was late, so I missed the bus.” Or: “I was late; I missed the bus.”

Essential vs. non-essential clauses

Use commas around extra information that can be removed without changing which person or thing is identified.

Non-essential

My laptop, which I bought last year, is still fast.

The clause adds extra information.

Essential

The laptop that I bought last year is still fast.

The clause identifies which laptop; no commas in this common restrictive pattern.

Punctuation conventions can vary by dialect and style guide. Use a consistent style and preserve the intended meaning.

It's = it is / it has: It's raining. It's been a long day.
Whose asks about possession; who's means who is or who has.

Do not use apostrophes to make ordinary plurals: “apple's” is not the plural of apple; write “apples.”

Quotation marks and reported speech

Use quotation marks to show the exact words spoken or quoted. Punctuation placement around quotation marks differs somewhat between American and British styles.

Direct speech: Maya said, “I am ready.”
Question: He asked, “Are you ready?”
Quote within a quote: “She said, ‘Wait here,’ and left.”

In reported speech, quotation marks are usually not needed: Maya said that she was ready. Do not place quotation marks around every ordinary word you want to emphasise.

Parentheses, brackets, and ellipses

  • Parentheses ( ) add extra information: The exam (scheduled for Friday) starts at nine.
  • Square brackets [ ] can add an editor's clarification inside a quotation.
  • Ellipsis (…) can indicate omitted text or a trailing-off thought. Avoid using it in place of ordinary punctuation everywhere.

4. Capitalization rules

Sentence beginnings

Today is a good day.

Pronoun “I”

My friend and I are studying.

Names and places

Raj, India, Bengaluru

Days and months

Monday, October

Languages and nationalities

English, Indian, Japanese

Titles and names

Dr Rao, Professor Sen; capitalize titles according to their use and style.

In English, common nouns are not normally capitalized unless they begin a sentence or are part of a proper name: the river but the River Thames.
