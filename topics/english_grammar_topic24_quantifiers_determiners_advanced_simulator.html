Quantifiers and Determiners — Advanced

Learn how to describe quantity precisely: few or a few, little or a little, less or fewer, each or every, and much more. Work through examples, practise sentence choices, and finish with 40 multiple-choice questions.

1. What are quantifiers and determiners?

A determiner comes before a noun to help identify it or show whose/which one: the book, my phone, those people. A quantifier tells us how much or how many: some water, many books, enough time. Quantifiers are one kind of determiner.

Quantity

many students, much time, several ideas

Amount

a little milk, enough money, less noise

Identity

this chair, those houses, each answer

Possession

my bag, their project, Raj’s notebook

Countable vs. uncountable nouns

CountableUncountable (usually)
one apple, two apples; a chair; several questionswater, advice, information, furniture, equipment, homework
How many apples?How much water?
fewer applesless water
Say a piece of advice, two pieces of information, or three items of furniture. These nouns are not normally pluralised as “advices,” “informations,” or “furnitures” in standard English.

Some nouns can be countable or uncountable depending on meaning: “three coffees” can mean three cups of coffee.

Quick decision method

  1. Identify the noun.
  2. Ask: can I count it as individual units in this meaning?
  3. Choose a quantifier that fits the noun and the intended meaning.
  4. Check whether the sentence is positive, negative, or a question, since some choices are more natural in certain contexts.

2. Few, a few, little, a little

ExpressionUseMeaning / example
few + plural countableSmall number; often negativeFew people understood. (= almost none)
a few + plural countableSome; enough to mentionA few people understood. (= some)
little + uncountableSmall amount; often negativeWe have little time. (= almost none)
a little + uncountableSome amountWe have a little time. (= some)
Compare: “I have few friends here” suggests almost none. “I have a few friends here” means I have some friends.

Much, many, a lot of, plenty of

  • many + plural countable: many books, many tasks.
  • much + uncountable: much work, much information. It is especially common in questions and negatives.
  • a lot of / lots of + either: a lot of books; a lot of work.
  • plenty of + either: more than enough; plenty of chairs, plenty of time.
Natural: “How much time do we have?” / “We don’t have much time.” / “We have a lot of time.” In positive everyday statements, “a lot of” is often more natural than “much.”

Less vs. fewer

Fewer is traditionally used with plural countable nouns; less with uncountable nouns.

Fewer

fewer mistakes
fewer people
fewer emails

Less

less water
less stress
less time

In everyday usage, “less” is also used with amounts, distances, time, money, and some measures: less than ten kilometres, less than $20, less than an hour.

3. Each, every, all, both, either, neither

WordMeaning and grammarExample
eachIndividual members; usually singular noun and singular verbEach student has a card.
everyAll members considered one by one; singular noun/verbEvery student has a card.
allThe whole group or amountAll the students are here. All the water is gone.
bothThe two together; plural verb with a plural subjectBoth answers are correct.
eitherOne or the other of twoYou can choose either option.
neitherNot one and not the other of twoNeither answer is correct.
noneNot any / not one; verb agreement depends on context and styleNone of the information is missing. / None of the students are ready.
Remember: Each of the players is ready. “Each” is singular in this pattern. Both of the players are ready.

Either and neither usually refer to two choices. In formal writing, use them carefully when a group contains more than two.

Another, other, the other, others

another

One more or a different one: “Could I have another cup?”

other + noun

Different/additional things: “Other students arrived later.”

the other

The remaining one of two or a known set: “One shoe is here; the other is missing.”

others

Other people/things, without a noun after it: “Some agreed; others disagreed.”

Do not say “another students.” Say “another student” or “other students.”

4. Enough, too much, too many, plenty of

  • enough + noun: enough chairs, enough time.
  • adjective/adverb + enough: old enough, quickly enough.
  • too much + uncountable: too much sugar.
  • too many + plural countable: too many messages.
  • too few / too little: not as many/much as needed.
  • plenty of: a large, sufficient amount or number.
“We have enough chairs.” But “The room is big enough.” Notice where enough appears.

Some, any, no, every

WordTypical patternExample
someOften positive statements; offers and requestsI bought some apples. Would you like some tea?
anyOften questions and negatives; also “whichever” in positive statementsDo you have any questions? I don’t have any money. Choose any seat.
noBefore a noun; means not anyThere is no milk left.
everyAll individual members; singular nounEvery room has a window.

“Some” is common in offers and requests when a positive answer is expected: Would you like some coffee? “Any” can mean it does not matter which: Any student can apply.

Determiners and agreement

  • This/that + singular or uncountable noun: this book, that advice.
  • These/those + plural noun: these books, those people.
  • Many a + singular noun + singular verb: Many a student has asked. (formal/literary)
  • A number of + plural noun + plural verb: A number of students are absent.
  • The number of + plural noun + singular verb: The number of students is rising.
