Advanced Writing Grammar

Bring everything together: grammar, paragraph flow, tone, transitions, sentence variety, consistency, and proofreading. This is the final topic in your English Grammar syllabus.

1. Grammar that supports good writing

Advanced writing grammar is not about using the most complicated sentence. It is about choosing a structure that makes your meaning accurate, clear, and appropriate for your reader.

Accuracy

Control the basics

Keep tense, agreement, pronouns, punctuation, and clause structure correct.

Clarity

Make relationships obvious

Show whether ideas contrast, explain, add information, or lead to a result.

Audience

Choose the right tone

Use concise, polite wording for professional writing and a natural tone for informal messages.

Flow

Guide the reader

Use paragraph focus, transitions, and sentence variety to connect ideas.

2. Cohesion and transitions

Cohesion means the parts of a text connect clearly. Transitions signal the relationship between ideas.

Addition

also, furthermore, in addition

The tool is affordable. In addition, it is easy to use.

Contrast

however, nevertheless, whereas, although

The task was difficult. However, we finished on time.

Cause / result

because, therefore, consequently, as a result

The server failed. As a result, the site went offline.

Example / clarification

for example, specifically, in other words

Some tasks are automated. For example, backups run nightly.

Sequence

first, next, subsequently, finally

First, install the package. Next, configure it.

Conclusion

in summary, overall, ultimately

Overall, the new process saves time.
Do not use a transition just because it sounds formal. “However” signals contrast, while “therefore” signals a result. The relationship between your ideas must make sense.

Punctuation: “However” between independent clauses often follows a semicolon and takes a comma: “The plan was risky; however, it worked.” Do not join two complete sentences with only a comma.

3. Tone, sentence variety, and consistency

Formal vs. informal

Informal: “Hey, send me the file.”

Polite professional: “Could you please send me the file?”

Match tone to reader and purpose.

Sentence variety

Short: The build failed.

Combined: The build failed because a dependency was missing.

Varied: The dependency was missing. As a result, the build failed.

Consistent tense

Incorrect: She opened the file and checks the data.

Correct: She opened the file and checked the data.

Pronoun clarity

Unclear: Sam told Lee that he had won.

Clearer: Sam told Lee, “I have won.”

Name the person when a pronoun could refer to more than one person.

Sentence variety tip: Mix simple, compound, and complex sentences. Do not make every sentence long. A short sentence can emphasize an important point.
Professional writing: Prefer specific verbs, direct wording, and polite requests. Avoid unnecessary jargon, excessive exclamation marks, and long openings that delay the main point.

4. Editing workshop

Use a repeatable editing process rather than trying to fix everything at once.

Pass 1: Meaning

Does each paragraph have one main purpose? Are claims supported and ideas in a logical order?

Pass 2: Structure

Check sentence boundaries, clauses, parallelism, comparisons, and transitions.

Pass 3: Grammar

Check tense, subject–verb agreement, articles, prepositions, and pronoun reference.

Pass 4: Mechanics

Check punctuation, spelling, capitalization, formatting, and consistent names or terms.

Before: “Our app is easy to use, it also saves time. Due to the fact that users need speed we made it faster. The team test it yesterday and will release it soon.”
After: “Our app is easy to use, and it saves time. Because users need speed, we improved its performance. The team tested it yesterday and will release it soon.”

The revision fixes the comma splice, reduces wordiness, adds a comma after an introductory clause, and makes the tense consistent.

5. Writing practice

Choose a task, write a short response, and compare it with the guidance. This is a self-review tool, not an automatic full essay grader.

Final proofreading checklist

  • Is the main purpose clear from the beginning?
  • Does each paragraph focus on one main idea?
  • Do transitions match the logical relationship?
  • Are tenses, pronouns, and terms consistent?
  • Are lists parallel and comparisons logical?
  • Have I removed unnecessary words without losing meaning?
  • Are punctuation and sentence boundaries correct?
  • Does the tone fit the reader and situation?
