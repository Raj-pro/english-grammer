Advanced Verb Structures

Go beyond basic tenses. Learn verb patterns, gerunds and infinitives, causative structures, perfect infinitives, and advanced auxiliary combinations with examples and guided practice.

1. Verb patterns: the verb controls what comes next

Many English verbs are followed by a particular structure. Learn the verb and its pattern together. A sentence can sound wrong even when every individual word is familiar.

Verb + gerund (-ing)

enjoy reading
avoid making mistakes
consider changing jobs
finish writing
suggest taking a break

Verb + to-infinitive

want to learn
decide to leave
hope to win
plan to travel
promise to help

Verb + object + to-infinitive

want me to stay
ask him to wait
encourage them to apply
persuade her to join

Verb + bare infinitive

can swim
must leave
let him go
make them wait

Correct: She enjoys reading. / She decided to read.
Incorrect: She enjoys to read. / She decided reading.

Verbs that change meaning

PatternMeaningExample
remember + -ingRecall a past actionI remember locking the door.
remember + to-infinitiveRemember a task you must doRemember to lock the door.
stop + -ingQuit an activityHe stopped smoking.
stop + to-infinitivePause one activity in order to do anotherHe stopped to answer the phone.
try + -ingExperiment with a possible solutionTry restarting the computer.
try + to-infinitiveMake an effortI tried to lift the box.
regret + -ingFeel sorry about a past actionI regret saying that.
regret + to-infinitiveFormal: be sorry to announce/sayWe regret to inform you…

Common patterns to memorise

Suggest: suggest doing something / suggest that someone do or should do something. Avoid “suggest someone to do” in standard English.

Make: make someone do something (bare infinitive). In the passive: someone is made to do something.

Help: help someone do something / help someone to do something. Both are acceptable.

Would like: would like to do something; would like someone to do something.

2. Causative structures

Causatives describe causing, arranging, allowing, or requiring another person to do something.

StructureMeaningExample
make + object + bare infinitiveForce or causeThe coach made us run.
let + object + bare infinitiveAllowThey let me leave early.
have + object + bare infinitiveArrange/request a person to do somethingI had the technician check the router.
get + object + to-infinitivePersuade/manage to arrangeI got him to check the router.
have/get + object + past participleArrange for a service/action to be doneI had my laptop repaired.
be made + to-infinitivePassive of makeWe were made to wait.
Compare: “I repaired my laptop” (I did the repair). “I had my laptop repaired” (someone repaired it for me).

Practice the contrast

Let

My parents let me go.

Not: let me to go.

Make

The joke made everyone laugh.

Passive: Everyone was made to laugh.

Get

She got her brother to help.

Get + object + to-infinitive.

Have something done

We had the windows cleaned.

Use a past participle for the service/action.

3. Perfect and continuous infinitives

FormTypical meaningExample
to doSimple infinitiveShe hopes to win.
to be doingAction in progress / simultaneous actionHe seems to be sleeping.
to have doneAction before the main verb or reference timeShe seems to have forgotten.
to have been doingOngoing activity before a reference timeHe appears to have been working all night.
to be donePassive infinitiveThe form needs to be signed.
to have been donePerfect passive infinitiveThe work seems to have been completed.
“She seems to have left” suggests her leaving happened before the time when she seems a certain way.

Auxiliary combinations

Auxiliary verbs combine to show tense, aspect, voice, and modality. The order is usually:

modal + have + been + -ing / past participle
She might have been working. (modal + perfect + continuous)
The file should have been saved. (modal + perfect + passive)
  • must have + past participle: strong deduction about the past — “He must have forgotten.”
  • might/could have + past participle: possibility — “They might have missed the bus.”
  • should have + past participle: past advice, expectation, or regret — “I should have called.”
  • needn't have + past participle: an action was done but was unnecessary — “You needn't have bought milk.”

Participle clauses and reduced forms

Participle clauses can make writing concise when the subject is understood and logically matches the main clause.

Full: After she finished the report, she sent it.
Reduced: Having finished the report, she sent it.
Passive: Written in clear language, the guide is easy to follow.

Make sure the implied subject of the participle clause is the same as the subject of the main clause. Avoid dangling forms such as “Walking into the room, the lights were bright.”

4. Meaning, voice, and formality

Used to + base verb

Past habit or state that is no longer true: I used to live in Delhi.

Be used to + noun/-ing

Be accustomed to: I am used to waking up early.

Get used to + noun/-ing

Become accustomed to: I am getting used to the new schedule.

Had better + base verb

Strong advice: You had better leave now.

Would rather + base verb

Preference: I'd rather stay home.

Prefer

Prefer doing A to doing B; prefer to do A rather than do B.

Common traps

  • “I am used to working late” (accustomed to), but “I used to work late” (past habit).
  • “I'd rather go now,” not “I'd rather to go now.”
  • “You had better check the details,” not “had better to check.”
  • “The manager made us stay”; passive: “We were made to stay.”
  • “I prefer tea to coffee” or “I prefer drinking tea to drinking coffee.”
