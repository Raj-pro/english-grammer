Modal Verbs

Learn how can, could, may, might, must, should, would, will, and ought to express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, advice, and polite requests.

What is a modal verb?

A modal verb is a helping verb that changes the meaning of the main verb. It can show ability, possibility, permission, advice, or obligation.

AbilityI can swim.
AdviceYou should rest.
PossibilityIt might rain.
ObligationYou must wear a helmet.
Basic pattern: subject + modal + base form of verb. Say “She can swim,” not “She can swims” or “She can to swim.”

Core grammar rules

  • Most modals do not change for he/she/it: “I can,” “she can,” “they can.”
  • Use the base verb after a modal: can go, should study, might come.
  • For negatives, add not: cannot/can't, should not/shouldn't, must not/mustn't.
  • For questions, put the modal before the subject: “Can you help?”
  • Modals do not take “to” before the main verb. Exception: ought to and semi-modal expressions such as have to.

Modal verbs by function

Can — ability / informal permissionI can drive. Can I sit here?
Could — past ability / polite request / possibilityI could swim at six. Could you help me?
May — formal permission / possibilityMay I come in? It may snow.
Might — possibilityShe might be late.
Must — strong obligation / strong deductionYou must wear a seat belt. You must be tired.
Have to — external necessityI have to submit the form today.
Should / ought to — advice / expectationYou should drink water. The bus should arrive soon.
Will — future / willingnessI will call you. I'll help you.
Would — polite request / hypothetical situationWould you open the window? I would travel if I could.
Shall — offers / suggestions (especially I/we)Shall we begin?
Needn't — no necessityYou needn't bring anything.
Mustn't — prohibitionYou mustn't park here.
Important: “mustn't” means something is not allowed. “Don't have to” means it is not necessary. They do not mean the same thing.

Positive, negative, and question forms

PositiveShe can drive.
NegativeShe cannot drive.
QuestionCan she drive?

Examples

AdviceYou should practise every day.
Negative: You shouldn't skip practice.
Question: Should I practise now?
PossibilityHe might come later.
Negative: He might not come later.
Question: Might he come later? (formal/less common)
Obligation with have toShe has to work today.
Negative: She doesn't have to work today.
Question: Does she have to work today?
With have to, use do/does/did for negatives and questions in the present and past. “Does he have to go?” not “Does he has to go?”

Commonly confused modals

Must vs. have toBoth express necessity. Must can sound like a strong personal rule; have to often describes an external requirement. Usage overlaps.
Mustn't vs. don't have toMustn't = prohibited. Don't have to = optional/not necessary.
May vs. mightBoth express possibility. Might often suggests a more tentative possibility, but the difference is not absolute.
Can vs. couldCan for present ability; could for past general ability or polite requests.
Should vs. mustShould = advice/recommendation. Must = strong obligation or necessity.
Would vs. willWill often expresses a future action; would often expresses a hypothetical situation or a polite request.
After most modal verbs, use the base verb: “She might come,” “He should study,” “They must leave.”
