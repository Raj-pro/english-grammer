Conjunctions

Learn how to connect words, phrases, and sentences using and, but, or, because, although, so, and more. Includes interactive examples and 40 practice questions.

What is a conjunction?

A conjunction is a word that joins words, phrases, or clauses. It helps your sentences flow instead of sounding like a list of separate ideas.

Join wordsI like tea and coffee.
Join ideas that contrastShe is tired, but she is working.
Give a reasonWe stayed home because it rained.
Show a resultIt was raining, so we stayed home.
Ask: What relationship do the ideas have? Addition → and; choice → or; contrast → but/although; reason → because; result → so.

Clause reminder

A clause contains a subject and a verb.

Independent clause“I was hungry.” It can stand alone as a complete sentence.
Dependent clause“because I was hungry” feels incomplete alone. It needs a main clause.

Example: “I made a sandwich because I was hungry.”

Coordinating conjunctions

These join ideas of equal grammatical importance. Remember FANBOYS:

F — forI took a jacket, for it was cold. (means because; formal)
A — andRiya sings and dances. (addition)
N — norHe did not call, nor did he text. (negative addition)
B — butI called, but nobody answered. (contrast)
O — orWould you like tea or coffee? (choice)
Y — yetIt was late, yet she kept studying. (unexpected contrast)
S — soI was tired, so I went to bed. (result)
When a coordinating conjunction joins two complete sentences, a comma usually comes before it: “I studied, but I was nervous.” No comma is usually needed when joining only two words: “tea and coffee.”

Subordinating conjunctions

These introduce a dependent clause and show how it relates to the main clause.

Reason — because, sinceI left because I felt ill.
Time — when, while, before, after, until, as soon asCall me when you arrive.
Condition — if, unlessIf it rains, we will stay inside.
Contrast — although, though, even thoughAlthough it was cold, we went out.
Purpose — so thatI spoke slowly so that everyone could understand.
Comparison — than, as…asShe is taller than I am.

Comma placement

Dependent clause first: “Because it was late, we went home.” Use a comma after the opening clause.

Main clause first: “We went home because it was late.” Usually no comma is needed.

Avoid the common error “Although it was raining, but we went out.” Use one structure: “Although it was raining, we went out” OR “It was raining, but we went out.”

Correlative conjunctions: pairs

Both…andBoth Neha and Ali attended.
Either…orYou can either call or email.
Neither…norNeither the teacher nor the students were late. (agreement can depend on the nearer subject in common usage)
Not only…but alsoShe is not only kind but also patient.
Whether…orI cannot decide whether to walk or take a bus.
As…asThis bag is as light as that one.
Keep the grammar parallel: “She likes both reading and writing.” Both items should have the same form. Avoid “both reading and to write.”
