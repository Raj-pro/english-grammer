Adjectives

Learn how describing words work, where adjectives go, how to compare people and things, and how to put multiple adjectives in a natural order. Includes interactive examples and 40 practice questions.

What is an adjective?

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun. It can tell us what something is like, which one, or how many/how much.

Qualitya beautiful garden
Sizea small room
Coloura blue shirt
Agean old building
Opinionan interesting book
Number/amountthree apples

Where do adjectives go?

Before a noun: She has a red bag.

After a linking verb: The bag is red. / He seems tired.

After verbs like feel, look, smell, taste: The soup tastes delicious.

Adjectives describe nouns/pronouns. Adverbs usually describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs: “a quick runner” (adjective) vs “runs quickly” (adverb).

Adjective or not?

AdjectiveThe happy child smiled.
Adjective after “be”The child is happy.
AdverbThe child smiled happily.
NounThe happiness was obvious.

Common adjective types

Descriptivea clever student; a noisy street
Quantitysome water; little time
Numberthree books; first place
Demonstrativethis pen; those houses
Possessivemy bag; their house
InterrogativeWhich subject? What colour?

Some grammar books classify words like my, this, and three as determiners rather than adjectives. For beginner learning, the key is knowing how they specify a noun.

Adjectives with linking verbs

After be, seem, become, feel, look, smell, sound, and taste, an adjective can describe the subject: “The music sounds beautiful.”

Say “The flowers smell nice” when describing their smell. “Nicely” usually describes how someone performs an action, as in “She sings nicely.”

Comparative and superlative adjectives

Use the comparative to compare two people or things. Use the superlative to identify the top or bottom in a group of three or more.

Short adjectives: -er / -esttall → taller → tallest
small → smaller → smallest
Adjectives ending in -yhappy → happier → happiest
easy → easier → easiest
Longer adjectivesbeautiful → more beautiful → most beautiful
careful → more careful → most careful
Irregular formsgood → better → best
bad → worse → worst
far → farther/further → farthest/furthest
Comparative often uses than: “Mina is taller than Riya.” Superlative often uses the: “Mina is the tallest student.” Avoid double forms: not “more taller” or “most happiest.”

Order of multiple adjectives

When several adjectives come before one noun, English often follows this order:

Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose → Noun
a beautiful small old round blue Italian wooden table

You do not need all these categories in one sentence. Usually, two or three adjectives are enough.

Naturala lovely little old house
Naturaltwo large black dogs
Naturala new Japanese sports car
Awkwarda wooden beautiful big table

Common adjective mistakes

Wrong: This is more easier. → Correct: This is easier.
Wrong: She is the most tallest. → Correct: She is the tallest.
Wrong: The flowers smell nicely. → Correct: The flowers smell nice.
Wrong: He is more better now. → Correct: He is better now.
Wrong: She has a bag red. → Usually: She has a red bag.
